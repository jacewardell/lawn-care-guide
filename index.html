Mowing

Taller grass, deeper roots

Mowing is the cheapest thing you can do to improve a lawn, and raising the height is most of it. Deeper roots reach water further down, which in a cool-arid climate is the difference between a lawn that holds through August and one that thins out.⁠[BASIC]

The setting

Set the deck to 3 inches and leave it there all season. USU's fertilizer guidance assumes a lawn kept at 2.5 to 4 inches,⁠[FERT] the calendar sheet asks for a minimum of 2.5 to 3,⁠[CAL] and the checklist says to set it at 2.5 to 3 and mow there all summer.⁠[ALM] Three inches satisfies all three, and it is the height USU names specifically for heat and drought tolerance.⁠[BASIC]

The 1/3 rule sets your frequency

Never remove more than a third of the blade length in one pass.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL] At a 3 inch cut that means mowing when the lawn reaches 4.5 inches, not on a fixed day of the week. In May that is every four or five days. In late July it can stretch past ten.

This is why the mowing strip on the year chart tracks the growth curve instead of a calendar. Growth sets the interval, and growth is not constant.

Leave the clippings

Mulch them back in every time you can. They return nitrogen and cut evaporation.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL] The size of the effect is the part worth knowing: returning clippings cuts the nitrogen you need by up to 50%.⁠[FERT] Bagging means buying fertilizer to replace what you carried to the landfill.

  • Mow higher in shade than in sun. Less light means the plant needs more leaf to work with.⁠[BASIC]
  • Sharpen the blade in March, before the season starts.⁠[ALM] A torn leaf tip loses more water and invites disease.
  • Mowing season runs March to November. Start when growth starts, stop when it stops.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL]
  • Came back from vacation to a hayfield? Step it down over several mowings rather than scalping it in one.⁠[BASIC] Doing it once is survivable. Doing it habitually is not.
The one exception, late fall

Drop the last mow of the year to 1.5 inches. Long grass mats under snow and invites winter and early-spring disease.⁠[CAL] This is the only time all year you cut low, and it is a single pass, not a new setting.

Sources differ

The calendar fact sheet says 1.5 inches for that final mow.⁠[CAL] The monthly checklist says 1 to 1.5 inches.⁠[ALM] The gap is small and the peer-reviewed fact sheet is the stronger document, so 1.5 is what this page uses. If you are already at 1 inch you are still inside published guidance.

Watering

Deep and infrequent, on a schedule that moves

Over two thirds of the water an average Utah home uses goes onto the landscape.⁠[IRR] The single biggest error is a controller set once in May and left alone until October. Demand changes by a factor of three across the season.

The shape of it

0.5 to 1 inch per irrigation, and change the interval, not the depth, as the season moves.⁠[BASIC] Deep and infrequent drives roots down. Daily sips keep them shallow and helpless in August.

Sources differ

The monthly checklist lists 1.5 to 2 inches per week for September,⁠[ALM] which contradicts both its own August entry (“as temperatures cool, turfgrass requires minimal irrigation”) and the calendar’s September interval of once every six days.⁠[CAL] Two of those three agree, and a peer-reviewed fact sheet outranks a web checklist, so this page treats the September figure as an error in the checklist. Water September like spring, not like July.

Timing inside the day

  • Early morning, when it is cool and the wind is down.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL]
  • Avoid 10 AM to 6 PM entirely. That water evaporates before it does any work.⁠[BASIC]
  • Puddling or runoff means split the cycle. Short repeated runs with an hour between them lets water move into the soil.⁠[BASIC] Slopes, sandy soil and clay all need this.⁠[IRR]

What the smart controller is and is not good at

A weather-based controller solves the problem this section opened with: it moves the interval for you instead of leaving May’s schedule running in July. That is genuinely the hard part, and it is worth having. What it cannot know is how much water your heads actually put down, and that is the number every runtime depends on.

  • Set the seasonal adjustment to work, then verify it against the interval table above. If your controller is watering September on a July cadence, the weather feed is not the problem, the program is.
  • Add rain and freeze sensors if the controller does not already have them. USU calls them inexpensive and simple to install, and they shut the system down when rain or frost arrives.⁠[IRR]
  • Turn on cycle-and-soak for any zone that puddles or runs off. Same principle as splitting a cycle by hand.⁠[BASIC]⁠[IRR]
  • Cross-check against the state ET guide. Utah’s Division of Water Resources publishes a weekly lawn watering recommendation from live weather-station data, and it assumes your system applies half an inch per irrigation.⁠[IRR]

conservewater.utah.gov/weekly-lawn-watering-guide

Free, and almost nobody does it

USU runs a Water Check program that measures what your system actually applies and tells you the runtime needed to hit half an inch.⁠[IRR] Every runtime number on this page is guesswork until somebody measures your heads. This is the single highest-value thing in this section.

cwel.usu.edu/watercheck

Or measure it yourself with catch cups

Put straight-sided containers around a zone, tuna cans work, run the zone for a set time, then measure the depth in each and average it. That gives you the zone’s precipitation rate, and the rest is arithmetic.

Derived, not sourced

The runtime formula below is ordinary arithmetic, not a USU recommendation. The USU sources tell you to measure your output and give you the half-inch target,⁠[IRR] and they point at county-specific sprinkler testing fact sheets not included in this page’s source set.

rate (in/hr) = average depth ÷ run time (hr)
runtime for 0.5″ = 0.5 ÷ rate × 60 min

Worked: cups average 0.2 inch after a 15 minute run. Rate is 0.2 ÷ 0.25 = 0.8 in/hr. Runtime for half an inch is 0.5 ÷ 0.8 × 60 = 38 minutes. Split that into two 19 minute cycles if it runs off.

The spread between your highest and lowest cup matters as much as the average. A wide spread is a coverage problem, and no runtime fixes it. See the head faults below.

Check the system monthly, not just at startup

Run each zone once a month and walk it.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL]⁠[IRR] Everything below wastes water and shows up as dry spots you will otherwise try to fix by adding runtime.

  • Fine mist off the heads means pressure is too high. Fix it with a regulator or pressure-regulating heads, not by shortening runtimes.⁠[IRR]
  • Mixed nozzle types in one zone is a guaranteed uneven lawn. Different nozzles apply water at different rates, so no single runtime is right for the zone.⁠[IRR]
  • Coverage should be head-to-head: each head’s spray reaches the next one.⁠[IRR] Anything less leaves rings of dry turf.
  • Overspray onto concrete is pure waste. Adjust the arc or change the nozzle pattern.⁠[IRR]
  • At spring startup, pull the last head on each zone and run it 1 to 2 minutes to flush grit before it reaches the nozzles.⁠[IRR]
Shutdown, and it is not optional here

Shut the main valve, open each zone to relieve pressure, then drain or blow out the lines and set the controller to off.⁠[IRR] A valley winter will crack anything holding water. Blow-outs happen in November.⁠[ALM]

Golden is not dead

Under drought stress the lawn goes gold and stops growing. That is dormancy, and it recovers when temperatures drop and water returns.⁠[BASIC] Keep traffic off it while it is dormant⁠[BASIC] and accept a less-than-perfect lawn for a few weeks.

Sources differ, and here is the resolution

Three USU sheets give three different fertilizer calendars. April, June, October,⁠[BASIC] May, September, November,⁠[CAL] and the table above.⁠[FERT] They are not really three answers. The table is the most specific of the three, it is explicitly adapted from the same document as the first, and its windows (spring, September, mid-October to early November) contain both of the others. This page follows the table.

The annual totals disagree the same way: 2 to 5 lb,⁠[BASIC] 2 to 4 lb,⁠[CAL] and 2 to 3 lb for intermediate maintenance.⁠[INORG] All three agree that a normal home lawn sits at 2 to 3 lb per 1,000 sq ft per year. The higher numbers are for sports fields.

The rule that beats every date

Make the last application when the turf is still green but no longer needs mowing.⁠[FERT] That is a thing you can see out the window, and it beats guessing at a calendar in a valley where the first hard freeze moves around by weeks. Use a quick-release nitrogen for it, which is what gives you early green-up next spring.⁠[ALM]

Why not July

Cool-season grass slows down hard in summer heat. Feeding into that slump promotes weed growth over turf growth and can push the grass into drought stress by over-stimulating top growth while the roots cannot keep up.⁠[FERT] Midsummer feeding is discouraged for everything except heavily used turf, and even then only in half-pound doses of a slow-release product.⁠[FERT]

The year chart is the same idea in one picture: the two feeding windows sit on the two growth peaks, and the gap between them is the trough.

Turning pounds of nitrogen into bags of product

Bag labels are quoted as three numbers, the percentage by weight of nitrogen, phosphate and potash.⁠[FERT]⁠[INORG] Only the first one matters for routine feeding, because Utah turf almost always needs nitrogen and rarely needs the other two.⁠[FERT]

lb of product = (lb N per 1,000 × area ÷ 1,000) ÷ (%N ÷ 100)⁠[INORG]

USU’s worked example: 2 lb of N per 1,000 using a 12-2-1 product over 1,400 sq ft needs 23 lb of product.⁠[INORG] Handy conversion when you have no scale: 1 pint of dry fertilizer is about 1 pound.⁠[INORG]

The burn ceiling

Never apply more than 1.5 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft in a single application.⁠[INORG] Above that you are burning the lawn. The standard dose is 1.0, so this is a 50% margin, and the way people blow through it is by spreading a full bag on a lawn smaller than the bag covers.

Quick release or slow release

  • Quick release is urea (46-0-0) or ammonium sulfate (21-0-0). Available immediately, spent in 4 to 6 weeks.⁠[FERT] Right choice for the late-fall application.⁠[ALM]
  • Slow release is coated urea, and it feeds over 8 to 12 weeks.⁠[FERT] Less leaching, fewer growth surges, so less mowing. Right choice for September, and mandatory if you feed in the heat at all.⁠[FERT]⁠[ALM]
  • Ammonium sulfate is the cheaper N source and more forgiving if you over-apply than urea is.⁠[FERT]

Spread it without striping it

Apply half the calculated rate walking one direction, then the other half walking perpendicular.⁠[INORG] This is the fix for the green-and-pale striping that shows up two weeks after a single-pass application, and USU calls it out specifically for turf.

  • Drop spreaders apply a narrow path very uniformly. Rotary spreaders throw 10 to 20 ft with less uniformity and less rate control.⁠[INORG] Either works if calibrated.
  • Calibrate it with 2 pints of kitty litter: run a straight path, measure the width and distance it took to empty, and treat 1 pint as 1 pound.⁠[INORG] Write the setting down.
  • Compare products per pound of actual nitrogen, not per bag. USU’s example: 29-3-4 at $1.52 per lb of N against 21-0-0 at $0.71.⁠[INORG]
Weed and feed, read the label

Fertilizers with herbicide mixed in are meant for turf, and they will stress ornamental trees and shrubs and kill most flowers and vegetables.⁠[INORG] Tree roots run well past the drip line and under the lawn. Buy the additive only if you actually need it that month.⁠[INORG]

Test the soil, then stop guessing at P and K

A routine test through the USU Analytical Laboratory runs $10 to $40 and is worth doing every 2 to 3 years.⁠[INORG]⁠[FERT]⁠[CAL] It will tell you texture, pH and salinity along with phosphorus and potassium. Valley soils are typically alkaline, around pH 7.5 in USU’s sample report.⁠[FERT]

There is no useful soil test for nitrogen, because N moves through soil too fast to measure meaningfully. Assume you need it every year.⁠[FERT]⁠[CAL] If the test comes back high in P and K, a nitrogen-only product like 21-0-0 is the correct purchase.⁠[FERT]

Weeds

One window you cannot miss

A dense, healthy stand of turf is the best weed control there is.⁠[CAL] Everything below is for when that is not enough yet.

Late March to mid April

Pre-emergent goes down before crabgrass germinates, not after.⁠[ALM]⁠[CAL] It works by stopping seeds from establishing, so once you can see crabgrass the product cannot help you and the season is gone. This is the one date on this page where being two weeks late costs you the whole year.

The two applications

  • First: late March to mid April.⁠[ALM] Targets crabgrass and spurge, the annuals.⁠[CAL]
  • Second: late May to early June.⁠[ALM] The calendar marks this one across June and July.⁠[CAL]

The calendar grid paints the first window wider, across March, April and May.⁠[CAL] That is not a contradiction, it is a coarser resolution: the grid works in whole months, the checklist narrows it to half-months. This page uses the narrower window, since being early costs you nothing and being late costs you everything.

Derived, not sourced

Turf literature outside this page’s source set usually ties crabgrass pre-emergent to soil temperature at 2 inches rather than to a date, because a warm March moves the biology forward. None of the six USU sources used here mention soil temperature at all, so no threshold is quoted on this page. The USU dates above are the sourced guidance. If you want to sharpen the timing, check a local soil-temperature reading before you apply, and treat the date window as the outer bound.

Broadleaf weeds, and the temperature that stops you

Dandelion, clover and the rest get a post-emergent while they are actively growing.⁠[CAL] The calendar windows are May and June, then September and October.⁠[CAL] Fall is the better of the two, because perennials are moving sugars down into their roots and take the herbicide with them.

The spray window

Apply between 60 and 80°F, and stop entirely above 85°F.⁠[ALM] Hot-weather applications damage the lawn you are trying to save. In the valley that rules out most of July and August afternoons, which is another reason fall is the better window.

The label is a legal document

Choose a product labeled for the weed you actually have and follow the label.⁠[CAL] USU puts this plainly: pesticide labels are legal documents, using a product inconsistently with its label violates federal and state law, and the applicator is the one legally responsible.⁠[CAL]

Insects and disease, by month

  • Cutworms in April and May, in turf and new starts.⁠[ALM]
  • Turf insects generally from May through October. Scout rather than spray on a schedule.⁠[ALM]
  • Preventative systemic insecticide only where damage happened last year.⁠[ALM] Not a routine application.
  • Turf disease shows up in September and October. Watch for it rather than treating ahead.⁠[ALM]

Grubs and snow mold get named constantly in lawn-care advice, and neither appears anywhere in this page’s six sources. The general guidance above is what USU actually says. For a specific pest identification, USU runs an IPM advisory and a plant pest diagnostic lab.⁠[ALM]

Aeration

Pull cores, not spikes

Aeration removes plugs of soil and turf, opening large pores that let air, water and nutrients move down.⁠[CAL] It relieves compaction and gets microbes working on thatch, which is the only thatch treatment USU recommends here.

The spec

Hollow-tine core aerator, 3 to 4 inches deep, tines 1/4 to 3/4 inch across.⁠[CAL] Go over the lawn once in one direction, then again at 90 degrees to the first pass.⁠[CAL] Solid spikes are not what is specified: the recommendation is coring, which removes material rather than compressing it further.

When

Time it to active growth, which for bluegrass means early to mid spring and late summer into early fall.⁠[CAL] The calendar grid marks April, May and September.⁠[CAL] The monthly checklist widens that to April through June and September through October.⁠[ALM] Fall is the better half, for the same reasons fall overseeding wins.

Do not aerate a dormant summer lawn. The whole point is that the grass is growing fast enough to knit the holes closed.

If you aerate in spring

Consider a pre-emergent afterward where weeds have been a problem.⁠[CAL] Opening the soil is an invitation to weed seed as much as to grass seed. Note that this pulls in the opposite direction from overseeding, which is the next section.

Overseeding

Late August, not April

This is the clearest recommendation in the whole USU set, and it is the opposite of what most people do.

The window

Late summer is the optimum seeding time in northern Utah.⁠[CAL] The grid marks April and May as good and August and September as best.⁠[BASIC]⁠[CAL] Allow 4 to 6 weeks of growing time before the first hard frost.⁠[CAL]⁠[ALM]

Three reasons, all of them from the same paragraph

  • Warm soil germinates seed faster than the cold wet soil of spring.⁠[CAL]
  • Fall seeding gets two cool growing seasons, this fall and next spring, to establish before summer heat arrives.⁠[CAL] Spring seeding gets a few weeks and then hits July.
  • Annual weed pressure is lower in late summer and early fall than in spring.⁠[CAL]
Derived, not sourced

There is a fourth reason the sources imply but do not state. Pre-emergent herbicide works by stopping seeds from establishing,⁠[CAL] and it cannot tell crabgrass seed from bluegrass seed. So a spring overseed and a spring pre-emergent are mutually exclusive: doing both means one of them was wasted money. Overseeding in the fall lets you keep the late-March pre-emergent window, which is the one window on this page you cannot afford to skip.

Method

  • A slit or disk seeder drops seed into cuts in the soil, which is the good version.⁠[CAL]
  • Or seed straight after aeration and let the cores act as the seedbed.⁠[CAL] This is why the aeration and overseeding windows are the same weeks, and doing both in one weekend is the efficient play.
  • Seed needs soil contact to establish. Broadcasting onto an unprepared, unaerated lawn is how most overseeding fails.

The 90/10 tall fescue question

Worth separating what the calendar cares about from what it does not. USU treats Kentucky bluegrass and the fescues as one category for every operation on this page: seeding windows, overseeding windows, fertilization timing and aeration timing are all listed as “cool season grasses” with no distinction between them.⁠[CAL] Mowing height does not change either, since all the commonly grown Utah lawn species share the 2 to 4 inch band.⁠[BASIC]

So the practical answer is that a 90/10 turf-type tall fescue and bluegrass overseed does not move a single date or number on this page. Every calendar entry, every rate and every interval stays exactly as written. That is the useful thing to know before deciding.

What this source set cannot tell you

The six USU documents used here do not cover cultivar selection, blend ratios, seeding rates, or the agronomic differences between bluegrass and tall fescue. Anything you read on this page about why you might prefer one over the other would be unsourced, so it is not here.

The three questions the blend decision actually turns on, and none of them are answerable from this page’s sources: how the two species differ in rooting depth and water use, what lb per 1,000 sq ft to seed at, and whether a bunch-forming grass and a rhizome-spreading grass knit into an even stand at a 90/10 ratio. USU publishes a Turfgrass Cultivars for Utah fact sheet that covers exactly this, and it is referenced by the calendar⁠[CAL] but is not one of the documents behind this page. Get that before you buy seed.

Also worth knowing

  • Sod goes down almost any time you can water it, but never on frozen soil.⁠[CAL] Laid before freeze-up it needs protection from drying out over winter.⁠[CAL]
  • Repairing damaged patches follows the same August through September timing as a general overseed.⁠[ALM]
  • Overseeding bare or thinning patches can also be done in late winter or early spring,⁠[CAL] which is the one case for going early. It costs you the pre-emergent that year.

Troubleshooting

Brown, or actually dead?

Four physical tests, each taking under a minute, that sort most lawn panics before you spend money. Do these before you buy anything.

Derived, not sourced

These four tests are standard turf diagnostics, but only the fourth one is supported by this page’s six USU documents. The tug, screwdriver and footprint tests are not described in any of them. They are here because they are cheap, non-destructive and they tell you which sourced section to go read. Treat them as triage, not as USU guidance.

Test one

The tug test

Grab a handful of the brown turf and pull up. If it lifts away like loose carpet with no roots holding it, something ate the roots. That is not a watering problem and more water will not fix it. If it holds fast and the crowns are firm, the roots are fine and you are looking at dormancy or a watering issue.

Test two

The screwdriver test

Push a long screwdriver into the lawn. It should slide in six inches with hand pressure. If it stops short, you have either bone-dry soil or compaction, and those need opposite responses. Water a small patch and retest an hour later: if it goes in now it was dry, so read Water. If it still will not, it is compaction, so read Aerate.

Test three

The footprint test

Walk across the lawn and look back. Healthy turf springs back. If your footprints stay pressed flat, the grass has lost enough water that the blades cannot stand up. That is the signal to water now rather than waiting for the next scheduled cycle, and it is the most useful thing you can learn to notice.

Test four

Straight edges or soft ones

Straight lines, sharp corners and repeating stripes are equipment, not disease. Living things do not grow in rectangles. Stripes point at the spreader, which is why USU says to split the rate and cross-apply.⁠[INORG] Hard-edged dry wedges and rings point at a sprinkler head.⁠[IRR] Soft, irregular, spreading outlines are the ones worth investigating biologically.

The whole lawn went gold in late July

Cause: drought dormancy, and it is normal for the grasses grown here. Under heat and drought stress cool-season turf stops growing and goes gold.

Treatment: nothing urgent. It recovers as temperatures cool and water returns. Keep traffic off it while it is dormant. Confirm with the tug test first, because dormant turf still has firm roots.

Source: ⁠[BASIC] for dormancy, recovery and traffic. The tug test is not sourced.

Pale and dark stripes, evenly spaced, a week after fertilizing

Cause: spreader skips and overlaps. A single-direction pass leaves alternating over-fed and under-fed bands.

Treatment: next time apply half the calculated rate in one direction and the other half perpendicular. USU names this fix specifically for turf. Calibrate the spreader with kitty litter and record the setting.

Source: ⁠[INORG]

Scorched strips or a burned patch right after feeding

Cause: too much nitrogen in one application. The ceiling is 1.5 lb of actual N per 1,000 sq ft, and the usual way past it is emptying a bag sized for a bigger lawn.

Treatment: water it in thoroughly to dilute and move the salt down. Recalculate before the next application using the area calculator, and buy for your actual square footage.

Source: ⁠[INORG]

Dry wedges, rings or crescents with hard edges

Cause: a coverage fault, not a water-volume fault. Sunken, tilted, blocked, clogged or broken heads, mixed nozzle types within one zone, or spacing that is not head-to-head.

Treatment: run the zone and walk it. A few degrees of tilt alone is enough to leave a dry spot. Raise sunken heads to grade, match nozzles within the zone, clear obstructions. Adding runtime to fix a coverage problem just overwaters everywhere else.

Source: ⁠[IRR]

Fine mist above the heads, and weak coverage far out

Cause: water pressure too high. Misting atomizes the water so it evaporates and drifts instead of landing where it was aimed.

Treatment: install a pressure regulator after the meter, or switch to pressure-regulating heads. This is a plumbing fix, not a scheduling one.

Source: ⁠[IRR]

Water pools or runs down the driveway during a cycle

Cause: applying water faster than the soil can take it. Common on slopes and on clay.

Treatment: split the cycle into shorter repeated runs and allow about an hour between them for water to move in. If the controller has cycle-and-soak, this is exactly what it is for.

Source: ⁠[BASIC] ⁠[IRR]

Thin, weak lawn that collapses every August

Cause: mown too low. Short grass has shallow roots, so it cannot reach water deeper in the soil, and in a cool-arid climate that is decisive.

Treatment: raise the deck to 3 inches and leave it. If you are stepping down from very long, do it over several mowings rather than one. Also check that clippings are being returned rather than bagged.

Source: ⁠[BASIC] ⁠[CAL]

Crabgrass everywhere by July

Cause: the pre-emergent window was missed. Pre-emergents stop seed from establishing, so once the plant is visible and growing they cannot touch it.

Treatment: nothing good this season. Mow high, keep the turf dense, and put the first pre-emergent down next year in late March to mid April with a second in late May to early June. Thickening the stand is the real long-term control.

Source: ⁠[ALM] ⁠[CAL]

Uniformly pale, yellowish green, growing slowly in spring or fall

Cause: nitrogen shortage, the most common nutrient deficiency in Utah turf. Uniform is the tell, since equipment faults make patterns and coverage faults make edges.

Treatment: apply 1 lb of actual N per 1,000 sq ft if you are inside a scheduled window. There is no useful soil test for nitrogen, so assume an annual need. Get a soil test for phosphorus and potassium before adding either.

Source: ⁠[FERT]

Weeds are winning even though I keep spraying

Cause: thin turf. Weeds are a symptom of open ground, and herbicide treats the symptom.

Treatment: USU is blunt about this: a healthy, dense stand is the best defense there is. Mow at 3 inches, feed on schedule, water deep and infrequent, and overseed in late August. If you are spraying, do it inside the 60 to 80°F window and stop above 85°F.

Source: ⁠[CAL] ⁠[ALM]

Lawn is slow to green up in early spring

Cause: usually just temperature. Growth resumes on its own schedule and the mowing season does not start until March at the earliest.

Treatment: wait. If you want earlier green-up next year, the lever is the late-fall application of quick-release nitrogen made after the final mow, not an early spring feed. Feeding before the grass is growing mostly feeds weeds.

Source: ⁠[ALM] ⁠[CAL]

Glossary

Every unit and acronym on this page

Lawn care hides a lot behind shorthand, and two of these (actual nitrogen, and lb per 1,000) are where most costly mistakes start.

Units and acronyms

N-P-KNitrogen, Phosphate, Potash

The three numbers on every fertilizer bag, always in that order, always as a percentage by weight.⁠[FERT] A 25-3-5 bag is 25% nitrogen, 3% phosphate and 5% potash, and the remaining 67% is carrier and other elements. Utah turf almost always needs the first number and rarely the other two.⁠[FERT]

Actual NActual nitrogen

The pounds of pure nitrogen you applied, not the pounds of bag you emptied. Every rate in this guide is actual N. Four pounds of a 25% product delivers one pound of actual N.⁠[INORG] Confusing the two is the single most common way people over- or under-feed by a factor of four.

lb/MPounds per 1,000 square feet

The standard unit for everything applied to turf. The M is the Roman thousand, not “metre” and not “million”. Bag labels and USU recommendations both use it, which is why knowing your square footage is the prerequisite for every product decision on this page.⁠[INORG]

ETEvapotranspiration

Water lost from soil by evaporation plus water pulled through the plant and released from its leaves, added together. It is the actual demand a lawn puts on you on a given week, and it is what weather-based controllers and Utah’s weekly watering guide are estimating from live station data.⁠[IRR] High heat, low humidity and wind all push it up, which is why valley demand triples between April and July.

Precip ratePrecipitation rate

How fast a zone applies water, in inches per hour. It is a property of your heads, nozzles and pressure, not of the controller, and it is the number that converts “apply half an inch” into “run for 38 minutes”. You cannot look it up. You measure it with catch cups or a USU Water Check.⁠[IRR]

pHAcidity or alkalinity

A 0 to 14 scale where 7 is neutral, lower is acidic and higher is alkaline. Utah soils are commonly alkaline, around 7.5 in USU’s sample report.⁠[FERT] It matters because roots can only take up some nutrients inside a narrow band, so a pH far outside it means fertilizer sits in the soil unavailable.

SalinityDissolved salt in the soil

Reported on a soil test in dS/m, decisiemens per metre. USU’s sample report reads 1.33 dS/m and calls that acceptable for cool-season turf.⁠[FERT] It matters here because fertilizer is itself a salt, which is the mechanism behind the burn ceiling.

USUALUSU Analytical Laboratory

The lab that runs the $10 to $40 soil test this page keeps recommending, and the reason you never have to guess at phosphorus and potassium.⁠[INORG] Recommended interval is every 2 to 3 years.⁠[FERT]

The words

Cool-seasonThe category your lawn is in

Grasses that grow hardest in spring and fall and slump through summer heat: Kentucky bluegrass, the fescues, perennial ryegrass. Warm-season grasses do the reverse, staying straw-colored until soil warms and peaking in midsummer. The Salt Lake Valley is transition zone and grows both, so every calendar is wrong unless you know which you have. This page is cool-season only.⁠[CAL]

DormancyGold, but not dead

The shutdown a cool-season lawn goes into under heat and drought stress. Growth stops and colour goes gold, and it recovers when temperatures cool and water returns.⁠[BASIC] Keep traffic off dormant turf.⁠[BASIC] The tug test separates this from dead.

Pre-emergentHerbicide applied before germination

Stops annual weed seed from establishing, so it must go down before the weeds appear.⁠[CAL] Once you can see crabgrass it is useless for that season. It also cannot distinguish weed seed from grass seed, which is why it and overseeding cannot share a spring. Post-emergent is the opposite: applied to weeds that are already up and actively growing.⁠[CAL]

BroadleafNot-a-grass weeds

Dandelion, clover, plantain, spurge and company, as opposed to grassy weeds like crabgrass. The distinction matters because the herbicides differ: a broadleaf product is safe on turf precisely because turf is not broadleaf.⁠[CAL]

Slow releaseCoated nitrogen

Usually coated urea, feeding over 8 to 12 weeks instead of 4 to 6.⁠[FERT] Less leaching, no growth surge, so less mowing. Required if you fertilize in heat at all.⁠[FERT] Quick release is uncoated urea or ammonium sulfate, available immediately and gone in 4 to 6 weeks, which is what the late-fall application wants.⁠[ALM]

Core aerationPulling plugs, not poking holes

A hollow tine aerator removes cylinders of soil 3 to 4 inches deep and a quarter to three quarters of an inch across, leaving open channels.⁠[CAL] This is what USU recommends. A solid spike compresses soil sideways instead of removing it, so it does not do the same job.

ThatchThe spongy layer at the base

Accumulated stems and roots between the green blades and the soil. A thin layer is normal. Excess blocks water and air, and the treatment USU names here is aeration, which encourages the microbes that break it down.⁠[CAL] Note that thatch is stems and roots, not clippings: returned clippings are mostly water and decompose fast, so mulching does not build thatch.

OverseedingSeeding into existing turf

Adding seed to a lawn that is already there, to thicken a thin stand or repair patches, as opposed to seeding bare ground. Best done August into September here, with a slit seeder or straight after aeration so the seed reaches soil.⁠[CAL]

Head-to-headThe coverage standard

Sprinkler spacing where each head throws far enough to reach the heads next to it.⁠[IRR] It sounds like overkill and is not: spray output falls off toward the edge of its arc, so overlapping is what produces an even application instead of dry rings.

Cycle and soakSplitting one watering into several

Running a zone in short bursts with time between them so water can move into the soil rather than run off. USU’s version is short repeated cycles with about an hour between.⁠[BASIC] Needed on slopes, clay and sandy soils.⁠[IRR] Most smart controllers have it built in under this name.

Provenance

Where every number came from

Six documents, all of them Utah State University Extension. Each claim on this page carries the id of the source it came from, and claims that are not from a source are marked differently on purpose.

How to read the markers

[CAL]Sourced. Grey, dotted underline. The claim is stated in that document. Click through to it.
[DERIVED]Adapted, not sourced. Amber, solid underline. Arithmetic, an inference, or a reading of a chart that the source does not state outright. Weaker than the grey ones on purpose.
[CAL]Sources disagree. Red, solid underline. Two or more documents give different answers, and the page says which one it follows and why rather than quietly picking.

There are four disagreements on this page: annual nitrogen rate, fertilizer timing, final mowing height, and September water volume. All four are surfaced where they occur.

[CAL]

Northern Utah Turfgrass Management Calendar

Peer-reviewed fact sheet, December 2017. Kopp, Caron, Muntz and Gunnell. The spine of this page: the month-by-month operations grid, the irrigation interval table, aeration specs, and the seeding and overseeding windows.

Trust: highest. It is peer-reviewed, it is the most recent of the calendar-bearing sheets, and it names Salt Lake County explicitly in its scope, so it is not being stretched to fit this valley.

extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/northern-utah-turfgrass-management-calendar

extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/irrigation-system-maintenance

Lawn Fertilizers for Cool Season Turf

Not from a source

Everything marked in amber, collected here. The catch-cup runtime formula is ordinary arithmetic built on USU’s half-inch target. The month boundaries on the year chart are this page’s reading of a USU curve that is labelled only “spring, summer, autumn”. The gallons figure in the area calculator converts inches over area at 0.623 gallons per square foot per inch. The pre-emergent and overseeding conflict is an inference from how pre-emergents work, not a stated USU warning. The tug, screwdriver and footprint tests are standard turf diagnostics that appear in none of these six documents.

And three gaps worth naming. This source set says nothing about soil temperature thresholds, nothing about grubs or snow mold specifically, and nothing about cultivar selection, blend ratios or seeding rates. Where lawn advice elsewhere leans on those, this page declines to invent them. USU publishes a Turfgrass Cultivars for Utah fact sheet that would close the third gap.

The precedence rule

When documents disagree, this page ranks them: peer-reviewed extension fact sheet beats extension web page beats manufacturer beats forum. Within extension sheets, the more recent and more geographically specific one wins. No manufacturer or forum source is cited anywhere on this page, and no bag label is treated as guidance, because bag labels exist to sell bags. Two commercial lawn-care articles were in the source folder and were deliberately excluded.

Sources. Every claim on this page comes from one of six Utah State University Extension documents, listed with links and trust notes in Provenance. Five are peer-reviewed fact sheets. The sixth, the monthly checklist, is a web page and is outranked wherever it conflicts with the others.

What is not sourced. Amber markers throughout, gathered in one place under Not from a source. In short: the catch-cup arithmetic, the gallons conversion, the month boundaries on the year chart, the pre-emergent versus overseeding conflict, and three of the four physical tests in troubleshooting.

Where sources disagree. Four times, and each one is called out where it appears rather than resolved silently: the annual nitrogen rate, the fertilizer calendar, the final mowing height, and September water volume.

What this page assumes. Kentucky bluegrass, a cool-season lawn, in-ground zones on a weather-based controller, in the Salt Lake Valley. The USU calendar it is built on covers Salt Lake County directly. A 90/10 tall fescue and bluegrass overseed changes none of the dates or rates, because USU treats bluegrass and the fescues as one category throughout.

The only dynamic parts are the “Right now” card, the highlighted rows and the current-month marker, which read the date in your browser, plus the area calculator, which stores your square footage in this browser and nowhere else. No server, no analytics, no cookies.
